Knee Injury Recovery: What to Expect

Last reviewed: November 2026

This page explains what recovery usually looks like after an ACL tear and other common knee injuries, with or without surgery. It covers each stage of rehab, what is normal, when to call your doctor, and questions to ask your physical therapist (PT).

Note: This information is for general education only and is not medical advice. Recovery times vary. Always follow the instructions of your surgeon and physical therapist.

Off crutchesUsually 1–2 weeks
DrivingOften 1–2 weeks (may be longer for the right knee)
Desk job1–2 weeks

JoggingOften 3–4 months, when cleared by your PT
Pivoting sportsUsually 9–12 months, after passing testing

Recovery by Injury Type

Typical rehab times for common knee injuries. Many people have more than one injury (for example, an ACL tear with a meniscus tear), which can change the timeline.

ACL Surgery Recovery Timeline

Rehab is based on milestones, not just time. You move to the next phase when you meet the goals of the current one.

Phase 1: Weeks 0 to 2

  • Swelling, stiffness, crutches, and possibly a brace are expected.
  • Rehab starts right away. Formal physical therapy usually begins within the first week.
  • Keep the leg raised and straight. Ice for 10 to 20 minutes every 1 to 2 hours while awake for the first few days.
  • Keep the incision clean and dry.
  • Goals: full knee straightening, less swelling, able to tighten the quad and lift the leg.

Phase 2: Weeks 2 to 6

  • Most people are off crutches. Bending slowly improves.
  • Common exercises: quad sets, straight leg raises, calf raises, stationary bike, balance work.
  • Goals: keep full straightening, bend to about 90 degrees or more, walk without a limp.

Phase 3: Weeks 6 to 12

  • Strengthening increases: squats, step-ups, leg press, single-leg balance.
  • Most people walk, squat, and use stairs normally by the end of this phase.
  • Goals: go down stairs with control, exercise without swelling the next day.

Phase 4: Months 3 to 6

  • PT visits often drop to every week or two, so more of the work is done on your own.
  • Jogging usually starts once cleared, followed by light jumping and landing drills.
  • Progress can feel slow. The knee often feels fine before the graft and muscles are ready, so avoid doing too much on good days.
  • Goals: strength getting close to the other leg, running without pain or swelling afterward.

Phase 5: Months 6 to 9

  • Agility work: cutting, changing direction, hopping, and sport drills without contact.
  • Return-to-sport testing often begins.
  • Goals: pass your PT's testing and feel confident in the knee.

Phase 6: Months 9 to 12

  • Gradual return to sport: practice first, then limited game time, then full play.
  • Many athletes take 8 months to a year to return to their previous level.
  • Some surgeons suggest a sports brace for the first year or two back.

After 1 Year

  • Strength and confidence often keep improving into the second year.
  • Keeping a short strength and balance routine two or three times a week is recommended, especially for pivoting sports.
  • Get checked if the knee swells, gives way, or locks.

Recovery Without Surgery

Not every ACL tear needs surgery. In one study of people with recent ACL tears, those treated with rehab alone and those who had surgery reported similar knee function and strength at two years. (The surgery group was younger and more often played pivoting sports.)

Rehab alone may be an option if you:

  • Do not plan to return to cutting or pivoting sports
  • Do not have the knee giving way in daily life
  • Do not have another injury that needs repair

Non-surgical rehab usually involves a few months of strength, balance, and control training. Some people try rehab first and choose surgery later. Talk with your surgeon about which is right for you.

Returning to Sport

Waiting and testing make a real difference. In one well-known study, each month of waiting before returning to sport lowered the reinjury rate by about half, up to 9 months after surgery. Patients with more equal quad strength between legs, and those who passed return-to-sport testing, were reinjured much less often.

Return-to-sport testing often includes:

  • Quad and hamstring strength, often within about 10% of the other leg
  • Single-leg hop tests
  • How well you land and change direction
  • A questionnaire about how the knee feels and how confident you are

Feeling nervous about getting hurt again is common and is one of the main reasons people don't return to their previous level. Returning gradually and talking with your PT or coach can help.

What's Normal and What's Not

Usually NormalTell Your PT
Mild soreness after exercise that settles by the next dayMore pain or swelling the morning after a session
Some swelling after increasing activitySwelling that keeps building over several days
Weeks where progress feels slowLosing straightening or bending you already had
Numbness near the incisionClicking or catching with pain
Feeling nervous about running or jumpingThe knee giving way or feeling unstable

When to Call Your Doctor

Call 911 if you have chest pain, trouble breathing, cough up blood, or pass out.

Call your doctor right away if you have:

  • Pain, redness, or swelling in the calf, knee, thigh, or groin (possible blood clot)
  • Signs of infection: increasing pain, warmth, or redness, red streaks from the incision, pus, or fever
  • Bleeding through the bandage, or an incision that opens
  • Numbness, tingling, or weakness in the foot, or a foot that turns cool or pale
  • Pain that does not improve with pain medicine
  • Later in recovery: the knee giving way, locking, or a pop followed by quick swelling

Questions to Ask Your Physical Therapist

  • What milestone am I working toward now, and how will we know I've reached it?
  • How much soreness after exercise is OK?
  • What should I do on days I can't finish my full program?
  • How does my injured leg compare to my other leg?
  • What do I need to show before I start running?
  • Which return-to-sport tests will I do, and when?
  • What should I keep doing after I'm discharged?
